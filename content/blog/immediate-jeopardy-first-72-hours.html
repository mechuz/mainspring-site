Immediate jeopardy: what has to happen in the first 72 hours

An immediate jeopardy citation starts a clock measured in days, not weeks. The sequence that removes the finding fastest, and the mistakes that extend it.

An immediate jeopardy citation is not a worse version of an ordinary deficiency. It is a different kind of event, with a different clock. Under the federal survey framework, immediate jeopardy means the provider’s noncompliance has caused, or is likely to cause, serious injury, harm, impairment or death to a resident — and that the situation requires immediate action. Three elements have to be present together: the noncompliance, the serious adverse outcome or the likelihood of one, and the immediacy.

That third element is the one operators underestimate. It is why a finding can be cited even when nothing has happened to a resident yet, and it is why the response has to be measured in hours.

The clock you are actually on

Three separate consequences start moving the moment the finding is made, and they move at different speeds:

  • Termination. If the jeopardy is not removed, the facility is on a short termination track — weeks, not months, from the last day of the survey. This is the one that ends an operation.
  • Civil money penalties. These can be assessed per day or per instance, and the per-day variety accrues while the jeopardy stands. Every day of delay has a price.
  • Denial of payment for new admissions. The revenue consequence usually arrives before the penalty does, and it compounds: an admissions hold in a referral market is not a tap you turn back on at the same pressure.

Removing the jeopardy does not remove the deficiency. It lowers the scope and severity so the facility leaves the termination track, and the underlying citation then proceeds through the ordinary plan-of-correction and revisit process. Those are two separate pieces of work, and conflating them is the single most common reason a jeopardy stays open longer than it should.

Hours 0–4: stop the harm and name one person

Before anything is written, the condition that creates the risk has to stop. If it is a medication process, the process stops and an interim process starts today. If it is a resident with unmet supervision needs, that resident is supervised now, by name, on a documented schedule. If it is a physical plant issue, the area is secured.

Then name one person accountable for the removal — one, with the authority to direct staff across departments. In most buildings under jeopardy, the administrator is simultaneously managing the surveyors, corporate, ownership, families and the clinical response. That is the point at which things get dropped. Separating the two roles — one person running the building, one person running the removal — is usually the difference between a three-day removal and a three-week one.

Hours 4–24: write a removal plan, not a plan of correction

A removal plan answers one question: what has been put in place, right now, that makes serious harm no longer likely? It is not the root-cause analysis and it is not the systemic fix. Those come next, and they belong in the plan of correction.

A removal plan that is accepted quickly almost always has the same shape:

  • The immediate action, stated as something that has already happened, with the date and time it happened.
  • Resident-level verification — every resident with the potential to be affected has been assessed or checked, by whom, and when. Not a sample.
  • Who was educated, on what, and how competency was verified rather than merely attested. Signed in-service sheets alone will not carry a removal.
  • Monitoring with a named job title, a stated frequency, and a stated duration — who audits what, how often, starting when.
  • Escalation — what happens, and who is told, when the audit finds a miss.

Surveyors generally want to verify removal in the building. That means the evidence has to be assembled and physically available — the audit tool with real entries on it, the competency checks, the schedule — not promised for later. A removal plan that reads well but has no artifacts behind it does not survive the walk-through.

Hours 24–72: make it hold

Once the jeopardy is abated, the pressure drops and attention drifts. This is the window in which facilities earn their next citation. Three things belong here:

  • The honest root cause. Not “staff did not follow policy.” Why was the policy followable on a Tuesday day shift and not on a Saturday night? Staffing, handoff, supervision, equipment, or the policy itself — name the real one.
  • The systemic fix, which is what the plan of correction will be judged on at revisit. It has to change the condition, not just the behaviour.
  • A monitoring tail long enough to matter. Audits that end in two weeks tell a surveyor the facility does not expect the fix to hold. Carry them through at least the revisit, with the results reviewed in QAPI and minuted.

What extends a jeopardy

In the buildings we are called into, the delay is rarely clinical. It is almost always one of these:

  • Submitting a plan of correction when a removal plan was required.
  • Education as the entire remedy, with no monitoring and no accountable owner.
  • A sample of residents checked when the citation implicates all of them.
  • No evidence assembled on site — the work was done but cannot be shown.
  • No single decision-maker, so corporate, clinical and the administrator each assume someone else owns the submission.
  • Letting the lower-level deficiency drift after the jeopardy lifts, then failing the revisit.

After removal

Expect the citation to remain at a reduced scope and severity, a revisit to verify the plan of correction, and penalties that may still be assessed for the period the jeopardy stood. There is a window to dispute findings through informal dispute resolution, and it is short — decide deliberately whether to use it rather than letting it lapse while the clinical work absorbs everyone.

Finally: the specific timeframes, penalty mechanics and removal expectations are set by current CMS guidance and interpreted by your state survey agency, and they are revised. Before you act on any of this, confirm the current requirements against the State Operations Manual and your state agency — and if the building is in it today, get someone who has removed one before into the room.
