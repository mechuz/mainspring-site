What actually moves your Five-Star staffing rating

The staffing domain is driven by payroll data you already submit. Why the raw hours-per-resident-day number is not the rating, and the handful of levers that change it.

Of the three domains behind a Five-Star rating — health inspections, staffing and quality measures — staffing is the one operators most often believe they cannot move without spending money they do not have. That is usually wrong. The staffing rating is calculated from payroll data the facility already submits, and in a meaningful share of buildings the number on Care Compare understates the staffing the building is genuinely providing.

That gap is not a rounding error. It is a data problem, and data problems are cheap to fix relative to hiring.

The rating is a ratio, not a headcount

The staffing domain draws on payroll-based journal (PBJ) submissions: hours actually worked by nursing staff, tied to resident days. But the rating does not compare your raw hours per resident day against a fixed national number. It compares your reported hours against the hours that would be expected for a resident population with your acuity, derived from assessment data.

Two consequences follow, and both are routinely missed:

  • A building with higher acuity is held to a higher expected figure. Raw hours going up does not automatically move the rating if acuity rose faster.
  • Assessment accuracy feeds the denominator. Under-captured acuity lowers expected hours — which sounds helpful until you notice it is the same under-capture costing the building reimbursement.

The staffing domain also reaches beyond hours. Turnover among nursing staff and among administrators carries weight, as does weekend coverage — which is where most buildings are thinnest and where the measure is therefore most discriminating.

The automatic downgrades

Some outcomes are not graded on a curve. A facility that fails to submit PBJ data for a quarter, or that reports days in the quarter with no registered nurse hours at all, can be dropped to the bottom of the staffing domain regardless of how well it otherwise performs. These are the cheapest stars in the industry to lose and the cheapest to protect: they are calendar discipline and RN coverage, not budget.

If you do nothing else after reading this, confirm two things this week — that last quarter’s submission was filed and accepted, and that no day in the quarter shows zero RN hours.

Where the reported hours actually leak

When we reconcile a quarter of payroll against what was submitted, the same leaks recur:

  • Job-code mapping. Staff whose hours qualify are mapped to codes that do not count, or are left out of the extract entirely. This is the single largest source of understatement we see, and it is usually invisible because nobody re-reads the mapping after the system was first configured.
  • Agency and contract hours. They count when they meet the requirements, and they are frequently the hours a building forgets to capture — precisely the buildings leaning on agency hardest.
  • Non-qualifying time counted in. The opposite error, and more dangerous: it inflates the number in a way that does not survive an audit.
  • The resident-day denominator. If census reporting and assessment data disagree, the ratio is wrong before any staffing question is reached.
  • Split roles. A director of nursing who spends part of the week in direct care, a staff educator, an MDS coordinator — the treatment of these hours has rules, and guessing is both a risk and a loss.

A sequence that works

  1. Reconcile one full quarter, line by line, from the payroll system to the accepted submission. Not a sample. Buildings find hours they genuinely worked and never claimed.
  2. Re-read the job-code mapping against current reporting requirements, and write down who owns it when the payroll system is next changed.
  3. Protect RN coverage every single day, and build the schedule so weekend coverage is not the shock absorber for weekday absences.
  4. Attack turnover deliberately. It is the slowest lever and the highest leverage — it feeds the rating directly, and it drives agency cost, survey risk and quality measures at the same time.
  5. Put the submission on a calendar with a named owner and a backup. Missed quarters are almost always a vacancy or a vacation, not a decision.

What this is worth

The rating is not vanity. Referral sources screen on it, families read it, managed-care and bundled-payment networks use it, and in several states it carries into contracting. A building that is genuinely staffing to a three-star level and publishing a two is losing admissions it has already paid for.

One caution. The measures, thresholds and cut points behind the staffing domain are set by CMS and are periodically re-based, which means a facility’s rating can move while its staffing does not. Before making a decision on any specific number, check it against the current Five-Star Technical Users’ Guide and PBJ policy guidance rather than against last year’s understanding — including anything you read here.
